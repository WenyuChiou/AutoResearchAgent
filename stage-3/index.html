Stage 3 design

What a plain Codex plan can miss

In a saved draft for this case, Codex proposed testing spending on unseen records and running a fixed-price control. It still needed data with prices and quantities for the same items. Here is why that matters.

  • A source may lack the data you need. A survey entry of $20 for food tells us what was spent. To test a price response, we need the price and how much was bought.
  • A comparison may change two things at once. If both the household age mix and the starting prices change, we cannot tell which one caused the difference.
  • A test may have no pass rule. “Check the predictions” gives no answer about how large an error is acceptable or when to leave price feedback out.

How Stage 3 builds a plan

  • 1. Check the evidence. Match each question to a source, a unit and a comparison. Search for a missing input. If it stays missing, narrow the question or record what is still needed.
  • 2. Build the plan. Link each question to its data, comparison and checks. Specify what stays fixed, what changes and what to measure.
  • 3. Review the design. Check the required fields, then use a separate AI review to test whether the steps can answer the question. Fix the affected parts and run their checks again.
  • 4. Ask the researcher to confirm. Review the sources, comparison and pass rules. Feedback goes back to the draft. Save the agreed version with its evidence and open questions.

If evidence or confirmation is still missing, the plan stays conditional. The formal review and confirmation path shown here is separate from the household case below.

Case · Can an LLM predict U.S. household spending by age?

  • Can a language model predict what U.S. households spend? Compare its dollar predictions for housing, food, transport, health care and entertainment with a simple spending rule.
  • Who counts as a household here? We use the survey’s consumer unit: one person or a group that shares major expenses. We group units by the age of the person listed as the reference person.
  • What would we simulate next? In a national U.S. scenario, raise the share of units with a reference person aged 65 or older. Test how spending and, if separately supported, prices could change under the same market rules.

P1 Research roadmap

  • Check spending first. Compare the language model’s predictions with survey spending from units it has not seen.
  • Check price response separately. Use matched prices and quantities before enabling feedback.
  • Keep the comparisons distinct. Compare decision rules on the same inputs. In the market scenarios, change only the age mix.

P2 Study workflow

  • Simulate a U.S. market scenario. Compare a reference age mix with a hypothetical older one. Fix profiles, budgets, starting prices, supply and the time horizon.
  • Separate the household rule from the market. The LLM or fixed rule requests purchases. The market allocates supply, updates prices and carries the state forward.
  • Run the comparison after both checks pass. Change only the consumer-unit age weights. Report demand and price paths as scenario results. A dated forecast would also require verified projected consumer-unit weights.

P3 How we would check the household model

We would compare predictions with survey records the models did not see during setup. The survey spending is the observed answer we score against. Published group averages show broad patterns; checking predictions for each unit needs individual survey records.

Evaluation · How good were the research plans?

  • What was scored? Full plans for the same U.S. spending question. Compare Codex alone with Codex using the Stage 3 planning process.
  • How many runs? Six paired runs, with one plan per setup in each pair: 12 plans in total.
  • Who scored them? One Codex reviewer read both plans in each pair and gave each plan five scores and written reasons. The reviewer could see which setup produced each plan.
  • What was the scale? Each criterion runs from 1 (very weak) to 10 (very strong). We adapted Goel et al.’s overall scale to five separate scores.

Why these criteria and checks?

[1] Goel et al. (2026) define the five criteria for research plans. Their appendix gives an overall 1–10 score.

[2] Si et al. (2025) use separate 1–10 scores for feasibility and expected effectiveness in a study with blinded human reviewers.

[3] Zheng et al. (2023) show that a language model judge can favor the first answer or a longer answer. Their results motivate hiding setup labels, swapping order and checking agreement with people in a later study.

Future Work

  • 1. Test Stages 1–3 with a new topic. Start from a short user prompt. Choose a direction in Stage 2 and check that its sources, limits and open questions reach the Stage 3 plan.
  • 2. Test an interrupted session. Close and reopen the project, return to the same plan and check that the system keeps the choice, evidence and latest saved version without running the same job again.
  • 3. Check the research inputs. Verify the U.S. survey records for the spending holdout. Look for matched prices and quantities. Keep the study at the spending comparison if the price evidence remains insufficient.
  • 4. Test the planner on more topics. Hide setup labels, score the plans in both orders, and ask people to score a sample. Report where judges agree and which criteria change.
  • 5. Build Stages 4–6 after the handoff is stable. Define how each later stage will take the saved plan, produce evidence and hand verified results to the next stage.
